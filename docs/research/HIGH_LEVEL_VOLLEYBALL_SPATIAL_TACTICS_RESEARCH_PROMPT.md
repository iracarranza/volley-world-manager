# Research Prompt — High-Level Volleyball Spatial Tactics

## Purpose

Research **volleyball truth only**.

Determine, as rigorously and comprehensively as available evidence permits, how high-level indoor six-player volleyball teams actually organize, position, space, coordinate, and reposition all six players through rallies.

This is not yet a VWM game-design, UI, abstraction, implementation, or attribute-design task. Do **not** decide what the player should control, how tactics should be represented in menus, or how VWM should implement the findings. Establish the sport model first.

The result should be detailed enough that a later design pass could distinguish:

- stable tactical structures from situational adaptations;
- coach/team instructions from player-level reads and adjustments;
- meaningful spatial principles from arbitrary coordinates;
- common high-level conventions from optional systems, stylistic choices, and context-specific solutions;
- what a well-organized team does from what an individual athlete must perceive and solve dynamically.

## Core question

> What positional structures, responsibilities, spacing relationships, movement rules, adaptations, and coordination principles actually govern six-player spatial organization in high-level indoor volleyball, before and during rallies?

Treat every named topic below as a **floor, not an exhaustive checklist**. Follow important adjacent questions wherever the evidence leads.

## Research standard

Prioritize, roughly in this order:

1. peer-reviewed volleyball research;
2. FIVB / Volleyball World / national federation coaching and technical material;
3. coaching education from established high-level programs and coaches;
4. systematic match/video analysis and high-quality technical breakdowns;
5. other sources only when needed to fill gaps, clearly labeled by evidential strength.

Prefer modern high-level volleyball. Distinguish men's/women's volleyball, level of play, era, and competition context when findings differ. Do not silently generalize a result from one population to all volleyball.

Cite factual claims. Preserve useful quantitative findings: distances, frequencies, distributions, efficiencies, zones, temporal relationships, formation prevalence, conditional probabilities, etc. Report sample/context limitations.

Do not search merely for named formations and repeat coaching diagrams. Recover the **rules and relationships that generate the geometry**.

Where literature is sparse, triangulate coaching sources and match evidence and mark the conclusion accordingly.

## 1. Serve receive

Build a complete model of high-level serve-receive spatial organization.

Research at minimum:

- 2-, 3-, 4-, and 5-passer structures and when each is actually used;
- rotational variants and how the six rotations alter practical geometry;
- how setters are hidden/released and how this changes receiver spacing;
- libero, OH, opposite, middle and setter responsibilities;
- seam ownership and communication rules;
- left/right/deep/short responsibilities;
- front-back relationships between passers;
- how passers divide court rather than merely where they stand;
- typical starting depths, widths and relative spacing where evidence exists;
- serve-origin effects;
- jump float vs topspin/jump serve adaptations;
- server handedness, angle, trajectory, velocity and known tendencies;
- receiver handedness/body orientation where relevant;
- protecting weak passers and expanding strong passers' responsibility;
- deliberate overloads, seams, short serving and tactical responses;
- how formations change after repeated server tendencies become apparent;
- how starting position differs from movement/read responsibility after contact;
- what constitutes good versus bad receiver spacing independent of eventual pass outcome.

Determine which properties are genuinely formation-level invariants and which are conditional adjustments.

## 2. Serving team's post-serve organization

Do not stop analysis at serve contact.

Research:

- server's defensive entry/recovery route;
- how serving location affects transition into base defense;
- teammates' initial defensive positioning during the serve;
- timing of movement into defensive base;
- whether/how teams pre-position according to expected reception quality or opponent tendencies;
- how the serving team's geometry changes as the opponent's first contact reveals likely attack options.

## 3. Blocking system and block geometry

Recover the spatial logic of high-level blocking, not just block technique.

Include:

- base blocker locations and responsibilities;
- read blocking, commit blocking, bunch/spread or equivalent systems where applicable;
- middle blocker starting positions and movement relationships;
- pin blocker responsibilities;
- block formation against quicks, slides, bic/pipe/back-row attacks and out-of-system balls;
- single/double/triple block conditions;
- closing geometry and spacing between blockers;
- line versus angle/crosscourt responsibility;
- hand/shoulder/body positioning insofar as it changes defensive court responsibility;
- intentional seams versus accidental seams;
- blocker decision hierarchy when multiple attackers remain available;
- setter front-row/back-row implications;
- opponent setter location and attack distribution;
- how reception quality constrains likely sets and therefore blocker positioning;
- how blockers coordinate with floor defenders rather than operating independently.

Explicitly identify what information becomes available at each stage of the opponent's possession and what high-level blockers can reasonably adjust to at that time.

## 4. Floor defense

Construct a detailed account of base defense and attack-specific defensive positioning.

Research:

- perimeter, rotational, middle-up/middle-back and other relevant defensive systems;
- base positions before set destination is known;
- movement from base to attack-specific positions;
- six-player responsibility relationships;
- defender depth and width;
- line/cross responsibility relative to block;
- hard-driven-ball versus tip/roll coverage;
- off-blocker responsibilities;
- setter defensive responsibilities;
- libero positioning;
- defender positioning behind/around single, double and triple blocks;
- defense against middle quicks, pins, slides, pipes/bics, setter attacks and out-of-system swings;
- how hitter approach, shoulder/body orientation, set location/tempo and known tendencies change defensive positioning;
- how imperfect or late blocks change floor-defense geometry;
- what defenders key/read and when they move;
- how teams avoid creating redundant defenders while leaving another attack lane empty.

Where possible, identify actual spatial relationships rather than generic statements such as "read the hitter."

## 5. Attack coverage

Research attack coverage as its own spatial system.

Include:

- known coverage structures (e.g. 1-3-1, 1-2-2 and other observed structures);
- prevalence by attack zone, attack tempo and level where quantified;
- first-, second-, and third-line coverage concepts;
- distance/depth relationships to the hitter;
- which players normally occupy each line;
- setter involvement after setting;
- coverage differences for quick attacks, pin attacks, back-row attacks and out-of-system attacks;
- effects of block size/location;
- transition versus coverage tradeoffs;
- circumstances in which fewer than five non-hitters cover;
- what predicts effective recovery of blocked balls;
- common structural coverage failures.

Do not reduce coverage to named formations. Determine the underlying principles that cause different structures to emerge.

## 6. Offensive transition and approach organization

Research how all six players spatially reorganize when a team gains an attacking opportunity.

Include:

- setter target/release behavior;
- hitter transition routes;
- approach starting points and spacing;
- middle transition;
- pin hitter transition from reception/defense;
- opposite behavior;
- back-row attacker preparation;
- decoy/availability positioning;
- how players avoid interfering with one another's approaches and setter path;
- differences between perfect-pass, off-net, emergency and transition offense;
- how attack tempo changes required spacing and timing;
- how players not selected as attackers reposition for coverage/continuation.

## 7. Continuation, scramble and broken-play organization

High-level volleyball is not only ideal first-ball structure.

Research what happens spatially after the planned structure breaks:

- setter takes first contact;
- non-setter must set;
- block touch changes trajectory;
- pancake/dive/emergency dig;
- overpass;
- joust;
- free ball/down ball;
- player displaced off court;
- hitter lands in another player's expected space;
- multiple players pursue the same ball;
- one player is unavailable/recovering;
- second/third contacts occur far from normal zones.

Determine what priority rules, role substitutions, communication conventions, spatial heuristics and recovery principles allow elite teams to reconstruct useful organization.

## 8. Free-ball and down-ball organization

Research whether these have distinct, strongly coached spatial structures.

Include:

- reception/first-contact responsibilities;
- setter positioning;
- attacker release;
- transition timing;
- differences between free balls, standing down balls, roll shots and other low-pressure incoming balls;
- common high-level patterns and meaningful variations.

## 9. Teammate spacing, interference and shared space

This is especially important.

Determine how real teams prevent six independently moving athletes from occupying or traversing incompatible space.

Research:

- spacing conventions;
- right-of-way / priority principles;
- seam responsibility;
- "mine/help" or equivalent communication logic;
- setter right-of-way;
- hitter approach corridors;
- blocker movement corridors;
- defensive pursuit conflicts;
- coverage conflicts;
- when one athlete yields or reroutes;
- how teammates respond when another player is already committed to a ball;
- collision/interference frequency if evidence exists;
- whether elite coordination is better characterized as collision avoidance, role/claim resolution, anticipatory spacing, route selection, or some combination;
- legitimate close convergence versus tactically bad crowding.

Separate **target conflict** (two players trying to occupy the same useful location) from **route conflict** (different useful destinations but intersecting paths) and **claim conflict** (two players attempting the same ball).

## 10. Rotation-specific spatial consequences

For all systems above, determine how rotation changes the geometry.

Do not assume a single mirrored template covers six rotations. Research:

- overlap/rotation-order constraints before serve;
- legal starting-position requirements and how elite teams compress/distort them;
- release immediately after serve contact;
- front-row/back-row role consequences;
- setter location;
- three-front versus setter/front-row attack implications;
- receiver availability;
- libero substitutions/role consequences;
- rotation-specific weak points and tactical adaptations.

Clearly separate rules-required pre-serve geometry from tactical post-contact geometry.

## 11. Temporal structure of positioning

For every major system, identify **when** positioning decisions happen.

Construct the best evidence-backed sequence possible, e.g.:

- before serve;
- server toss/approach;
- serve contact;
- early ball flight;
- reception contact;
- setter movement;
- pass trajectory/quality becomes apparent;
- setter body/set cues;
- set release;
- hitter approach;
- hitter contact;
- block touch;
- defensive contact;
- transition/recovery.

For each stage, ask:

1. What does each role know/perceive?
2. What positioning can already be committed?
3. What remains conditional?
4. What can still realistically be changed before the next contact?

Avoid hindsight geometry in which athletes react to information that was not yet available.

## 12. Static structure versus adaptive intelligence

Across all research, explicitly classify spatial behavior into:

### A. Stable/team structure
Examples might include base receive shape, defensive system, blocking assignment, or coverage principle.

### B. Context-conditioned team rule
A shared adjustment to rotation, server, hitter, attack zone, reception quality, etc.

### C. Individual read/interpretation
An athlete perceives cues and adjusts their own position/responsibility.

### D. Interpersonal coordination
Two or more athletes resolve seams, claims, coverage, routes or responsibilities together.

### E. Emergency adaptation
Structure has broken and players reconstruct playable spacing.

Do not force evidence into these categories if a better taxonomy emerges; improve the taxonomy and explain why.

## 13. Skill / intelligence differences visible in spatial behavior

Research what distinguishes better and worse high-level players/teams spatially **without designing VWM attributes yet**.

Look for evidence concerning:

- anticipation;
- visual search/read cues;
- initial positioning;
- movement initiation timing;
- decision quality;
- responsibility recognition;
- communication;
- coordination;
- tactical expertise;
- adaptation to opponent tendencies;
- consistency;
- experience;
- expertise differences between playing levels.

The goal is to establish whether elite spatial intelligence manifests primarily as better starting locations, earlier movement, better cue selection, better responsibility choices, better teammate relationships, fewer unnecessary movements, better recovery after disruption, or other measurable behaviors.

Do not translate these findings into game stats or formulas yet.

## 14. Quantitative spatial library

Build a reusable evidence table wherever the literature supports it.

For each finding capture:

- phase/system;
- role(s);
- situation/condition;
- spatial relationship or behavior;
- quantitative value/range/frequency if available;
- men's/women's/mixed;
- competition/skill level;
- source;
- confidence/evidence quality;
- limitations.

Useful quantitative evidence may include:

- starting coordinates/zones;
- inter-player distances;
- depth from net/end line;
- displacement before contact;
- movement onset times;
- coverage-line distances;
- block/defense relationships;
- formation frequencies;
- reception responsibility shares;
- attack/defense distributions;
- outcome associations.

Do not invent precision where sources provide only qualitative principles.

## 15. Contradictions and uncertainty

Actively seek disagreement.

For major tactical claims:

- search for counterexamples;
- distinguish convention from necessity;
- distinguish coaching doctrine from measured match behavior;
- identify differences between teams/systems;
- identify findings that have changed with modern serving/attacking trends;
- flag weakly evidenced common coaching claims;
- state when evidence is insufficient to justify a universal rule.

A useful result is not one universal formation. It is a map of **what is invariant, what is probabilistic, and what is tactical choice**.

## Boundaries

Do **not**:

- design VWM UI;
- decide player-control granularity;
- design tactical menus;
- map findings to VWM attributes or formulas;
- implement code;
- tune rally outcomes;
- assume existing VWM behavior is correct;
- retrofit research conclusions to current repo systems;
- recommend a simplified game abstraction merely because it is easier to implement.

Repo context may be inspected only if needed to understand terminology or avoid overwriting existing research. The research conclusion must be driven by volleyball evidence, not current implementation.

## Completion standard

Continue autonomously until the major spatial phases of a rally have been researched to source saturation: additional searching should mostly repeat known principles or add minor examples rather than reveal new major structural categories.

Before finishing, explicitly audit the document for:

- missing roles;
- missing rotations;
- men's/women's differences;
- ideal-system bias;
- first-ball bias;
- missing broken-play behavior;
- hindsight/perception errors;
- unsupported coaching folklore;
- over-rigid coordinate claims;
- unexamined teammate-conflict semantics.

Repo truth wins for repo facts; volleyball evidence wins for sport facts.
